# Yogurt Cottage Industry Laws

## What Is True (Short Answer)

- **Yes, Texas cottage law is broader now** and generally allows many foods, but it still has specific exclusions.
- **No, a "Super Gut / SIBO" fermented dairy product is not automatically legal to label as "yogurt."**
- **No, adding probiotic strains alone does not solve labeling or cottage-law risk.**

The biggest source of confusion is that there are **two separate legal questions**:

1. Can you legally **name** the product "yogurt"? (federal standard of identity)
2. Can you legally **sell** that type of product from a home kitchen in Texas? (state cottage-law + enforcement interpretation)

---

## 1) Federal Naming Rule: When Can It Be Called "Yogurt"?

Under the FDA yogurt standard (`21 CFR 131.200`), yogurt must be cultured with the characterizing cultures:

- `Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus`
- `Streptococcus thermophilus`

Other cultures can be added, but those characterizing cultures are the baseline for identity as yogurt.

If a product is fermented mainly with other strains (for example, `L. reuteri`, `L. gasseri`, etc.) and does not meet the standard, calling it "yogurt" in commerce is legally risky/misbranding risk.

---

## 2) Texas Cottage Law: Can You Sell It From Home?

Texas law expanded significantly (effective 9/1/2025, SB 541) and uses an exclusion model (many foods allowed unless excluded).  
One explicit exclusion is **raw milk and raw milk products**.

Important nuance:

- Using **pasteurized** dairy ingredients is different from using raw milk.
- But selling a **standalone fermented dairy product** (yogurt-like cultured milk) can still be a compliance gray area in practice, depending on product type, process, and regulator interpretation.

So the practical risk is not only raw-vs-pasteurized inputs. It is also how regulators classify the finished product (home-fermented dairy product) and whether it falls under rules that require licensing beyond cottage-food status.

---

## 3) Your Specific Product (SIBO/Super Gut Style)

For a long-fermented, probiotic-focused dairy product:

- **Name risk:** If it does not satisfy yogurt identity requirements, calling it "yogurt" is risky.
- **Sales risk:** Even with pasteurized half-and-half, standalone cultured dairy may still draw regulatory scrutiny under Texas enforcement/interpretation.

Adding the two classic yogurt cultures **might help identity compliance** only if the finished product truly meets that standard, but it does **not automatically guarantee** cottage-law sale compliance for a home-produced fermented dairy product.

---

## Practical Recommendation

Before selling, get a written determination from Texas DSHS (and local authority if applicable) using your exact:

- recipe and ingredients
- fermentation time/temperature
- final pH targets/testing method
- labeling language
- packaging and storage method

For lower labeling risk while you verify: use terms like **"cultured dairy product"** or **"fermented dairy product"** instead of "yogurt."
